§ 01

A quiet, forty-year study of the American body.

The CDC runs a continuous survey. Every two years it recruits a new, nationally representative sample of Americans — roughly five thousand per year — and subjects each one to interview, examination, and laboratory work.

You've seen its findings without knowing it. The obesity trend curves, the lead-exposure decline, the prediabetes prevalence — almost every large claim about American public health is, at its source, a NHANES analysis.

What makes it unusual: the data is measured, not self-reported. Participants don't tell CDC staff their weight — they step on a scale. They don't estimate their cholesterol — blood is drawn, shipped to a lab, assayed, returned. For a clinician, this distinction is everything.

§ 02

Five families of files. One key binds them.

Rather than publishing one giant table, the CDC breaks each cycle into modular files grouped by how the data was collected. You pick up only the files your question needs.

Every file shares a single column: SEQN, a sequence number assigned to each participant. This is the universal key. Whatever you combine — demographics with lab work, body measures with diabetes questionnaire — you combine on SEQN. Think of it as the patient identifier that follows one person through their NHANES visit.

§ 03

A variable name is a sentence, not a code.

Every NHANES column has a name that looks cryptic — RIAGENDR, BMXWAIST, LBXGLU. Once you know the structure, you can read them like a diagnosis.

LBXGLU
Source file
LB = Laboratory. Tells you which family of files this variable lives in.
Data type
X = measured value. D means derived, LC means "limit comment" (was it detectable?).
What it measures
GLU = glucose. The mnemonic — usually the first few letters of the physiological name.

Once you internalize the pattern, it reads plainly. BMXHT is "Body Measures, actual measured value, height." URXBPH is "Urine, actual value, bisphenol-A." The question numbers in questionnaire variables are literally the printed question numbers on the interview form — DIQ010 is "Diabetes questionnaire, question 10."

Clinical-analogy warning

The "77 / 99 trap" is the radiology-equivalent of trusting a report without looking at the window settings.

In many NHANES variables, the value 7 or 77 means "refused," 9 or 99 means "don't know," and these look identical to real answers. Forget to recode them as missing, and your average household income silently includes "99" in the arithmetic.

This is the single most common beginner mistake. The cleaning layer of your course will drill it.

§ 06

Empty cells are data, not gaps.

You're used to this from imaging reports: a missing measurement is never meaningless. Someone chose not to measure, or couldn't. NHANES has a small number of distinct reasons for missingness, and each has a different correct response.

01 · STRUCTURAL

Not applicable by design

A three-year-old has no marital status. A man has no prostate-specific questionnaire skip logic applied. These cells are empty because the question was never asked. Don't impute — subset your analysis appropriately.

02 · BEHAVIORAL

Refused or don't know

Coded as 7, 77, 777 or 9, 99, 999. These look like real numbers but are not. Always recode to missing before analysis.

03 · OPERATIONAL

Exam not completed

Participant left the exam trailer early; equipment malfunctioned; lab sample inadequate. NHANES flags these in a companion status column (e.g. BMDSTATS). Always inspect the status variable.

04 · ANALYTICAL

Below detection limit

Common in environmental chemistry — the assay couldn't see the chemical because concentration was too low. Flagged in a paired LC column. Standard practice: substitute limit ÷ √2 rather than drop.

§ 08

What to carry into session one.

You don't need to memorize any of the file names or codes. But a handful of mental models, carried in, will let you move quickly once the course begins.

KEEP IN MIND

Everything pivots on SEQN

It is the participant ID. Every merge, every filter, every subset happens through it. If you're ever confused about why a join produced too many or too few rows, come back to SEQN first.

KEEP IN MIND

Raw is in codes, meaning is in the codebook

You will never do analysis on the raw numeric codes directly. First you read the codebook for every variable you use; then you decide what to recode; only then does analysis begin.

KEEP IN MIND

Missingness has a reason

Before you drop rows or impute values, ask why the cell is empty. The course will teach you to read the status columns that NHANES always ships alongside measurements.

KEEP IN MIND

Files are topical, not monolithic

You'll never download "NHANES." You'll download the three or four files your question needs and assemble them on SEQN. This is modular by design, and you'll appreciate it by session four.

Everything in this pre-read will reappear in the course, hands-on — with code, with real files, and with your own research question.
